Research

I study how culture transformed a run-of-the-mill tropical ape into one of the most successful species on Earth.

My research lies at the intersection of archaeology and evolutionary anthropology. I use archaeological and comparative cross-cultural data, mathematical models, and simulations to study how culture evolved, how genes and culture interact, and how socially transmitted information allowed humans to occupy nearly every terrestrial habitat.

A central aim of my work is to develop macroarchaeology: a research program that takes advantage of the archaeological record's distinctive scale. Instead of asking a coarse-grained record to resolve the behavior of individuals, macroarchaeology asks questions about cultural change that can only be answered across thousands of years and large regions of space. This approach treats the scale of the archaeological record as a source of evidence, not simply a limitation.

Selected recent work

  • Human range expansionEmpirical comparisons with other mammals show that cultural evolution accelerated human geographic expansion by more than two orders of magnitude. PNAS, 2026.
  • The emergence of cumulative cultureA record of 3.3 million years of stone-tool manufacturing suggests a major shift toward cumulative technological culture by roughly 600,000 years ago. PNAS, 2024.
  • Cultural inheritance and human adaptationComparative analyses of 172 small-scale societies show that social learning is the main mode of human adaptation and that cultural history explains behavior better than local ecology. Proceedings B, 2015; Proceedings B, 2016.

Book

The Quality of the Archaeological Record
University of Chicago Press, 2019

The book argues that archaeology can make its most distinctive contributions by studying patterns and processes visible only at large temporal and spatial scales. Drawing an analogy with the rise of macroevolutionary paleobiology, it proposes a program for recalibrating archaeological questions and methods to the actual structure of the record.
